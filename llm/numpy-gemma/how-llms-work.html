How a language model answers

A walk through one answer, for someone who has never looked inside a model. The example throughout is Gemma 4 26B — the model this repository runs — and the question is the simplest one there is: “The capital of France is…?”

The one-sentence version. A language model does exactly one thing: given some text, it gives a score to every possible next piece of text. Then it picks one, sticks it on the end, and does the whole thing again. Everything else — the knowledge, the grammar, the reasoning — lives inside how those scores are computed.

The example sentence is processed as numbers from the first step to the last. It is worth holding on to that: there are no words inside the model, only lists of numbers that stand for words.

text→pieces→meaning vectors→ 30 rounds of refinement→a score per candidate →pick one→append & repeat
1

Cutting the text into pieces

The model cannot read letters. Its entire alphabet is a fixed catalogue of 262,144 pieces, called tokens. Before anything else, the text is chopped into those pieces and each piece is swapped for its number in the catalogue.

"The capital of France is" → The · capital · of · France · is → [818, 5279, 529, 7001, 563]

Common words get a piece to themselves. Unusual words are not in the catalogue at all, so they are built out of smaller pieces — the model might see “unexpected” as un + expect + ed. This is why a model can handle any text at all while still having a fixed vocabulary.

The analogy. Think of LEGO. There is a fixed box of bricks. Ordinary words are a single brick; unfamiliar words are assembled from several standard ones. Text in any language, or computer code, gets built the same way.

Nothing is lost here. This step is just bookkeeping — but it is worth knowing that a “word” for the model is often not a word for you, which is why token counts differ from word counts.

2

Turning pieces into meaning

Each number in the catalogue is attached to a list of 2,816 values — a coordinate. That list is the token’s position in a vast space of meanings.

The trick is that the coordinates are arranged so that related things land near each other. “France” and “Italy” end up as neighbours; “Paris” ends up near “capital”. The model’s whole sense of what a word means is where it sits.

The analogy. A map with 262,144 pins on it. Distance on the map is relatedness, and direction is a kind of relationship — one direction might separate countries from capitals, another might separate singular from plural.

These coordinates are not written by anyone. They are learned from text, and no human can read a single one of the 2,816 values. From here on, the sentence is just a trail of coordinates through this space.

3

Thirty rounds of refinement

The trail of coordinates now passes through 30 identical blocks. Each block does the same two jobs, in order, and then adds a small correction to what it was given.

The analogy. Sheets of tracing paper. Every sheet draws a bit more detail without erasing anything underneath. By the thirtieth sheet, the picture is complete.

Job A — Attention: deciding what to look at

To predict what comes next, the model has to work out which earlier words matter. Attention does this with a matching game played by every token at once:

  • Each token asks a question — “what am I looking for?” This is the query.
  • Each token also advertises what it contains — “here is what I am about”. That is the key.
  • Where a question matches an advertisement, the asking token copies the answering token’s content — the value.

By the time the model reaches the end of “The capital of France is”, the last token has collected “capital” and “France” into itself, and it has become something like “a specific kind of city, in a specific country”. That is the state from which “Paris” is the obvious continuation.

It can only look backwards. A token may attend to earlier tokens, never later ones — otherwise predicting the next word would be cheating.

It looks in 16 ways at once. The model runs 16 separate matching games in parallel, called heads. One head may end up tracking grammar, another the topic, another simple position. Their results are combined.

The window — the important part. In 25 of the 30 rounds, a token may only look back 1,024 positions. The other 5 rounds may look back at the entire conversation.

All 30 rounds, if they all looked everywhere
Rows are tokens asking; columns are tokens being looked at. The cost grows with the square of the length — doubling the conversation quadruples the work.
What the 25 sliding rounds actually do
A diagonal band: each token sees a short stretch behind itself. The cost grows in a straight line instead — ten times the conversation, ten times the work.

The analogy. Short-term versus long-term memory. Most rounds work with what was just said, the way you follow a conversation. Five rounds step back and consider the whole discussion. Restricting 25 of the 30 rounds is what makes long conversations affordable at all.

Job B — The experts: applying knowledge

Looking around is not the same as knowing. After attention, the token needs facts and patterns, and that is a second network — normally the largest part of a model.

This model splits that work into 128 specialist sub-networks per round and consults only 8 of them for any given token.

One round’s 128 experts. Eight are chosen for each token (orange). The other 120 never run for that token — but they all still have to be stored.

The analogy. A very large hospital. A patient is routed to eight specialists rather than being seen by all 128. Most of the doctors are idle most of the time, which is exactly the point: the hospital can be enormous because nobody visits everyone.

The router. The choice of which 8 is made by a small, simple calculation: score all 128, take the top 8. It is a hard choice, not a blend. Keep that in mind — it becomes important at the very end.

This is the model’s central economy: a 26-billion-piece model that only has to read about 4 billion pieces to produce one token.

4

The vote

After 30 rounds, the last token carries a single vector that summarises everything the model has worked out. To turn that into a prediction, it is compared against every one of the 262,144 tokens in the catalogue, and each gets a score.

The analogy. An election with 262,144 candidates. Nobody campaigns; each candidate simply gets a score based on how well it fits the current state. The winner is the model’s prediction for the next piece of text.

Scores are then squeezed into a limited range so that no single candidate can dominate absurdly — a detail that matters when the model is very confident.

5

Choosing one — sampling

The scores are not the answer; one has to be picked. How you pick is a genuine choice with visible consequences.

settingwhat it doeseffect
Always the top
(“greedy”)		 Take the highest score, every time. Consistent and predictable, but a single bad step is never recovered from.
Temperature How willing to consider the 2nd, 3rd, 10th choice. Zero means never. Higher is more varied and more creative — and more likely to wander.
Top-k / top-p Restrict the pool first: the best k, or the smallest set covering p of the probability. Prevents the very long tail of nonsense candidates from ever being chosen.

This model ships at temperature 0.3 with top-k 64 — a compromise. “Always the top” was rejected as the default, for a reason that only becomes clear in the last section.

6

The loop, and the notebook

The chosen piece is stuck on the end of the text, and the whole machine runs again to pick the next one. A three-sentence answer is hundreds of these rounds.

Here is the catch. Attention needs the keys and values of every earlier token. Those do not change from one step to the next, so recomputing them each time would be enormous waste. Instead they are saved in the KV cache and reused.

The analogy. Taking notes in the margin of a book. When you turn the page you consult your notes instead of re-reading from the beginning. It makes reading the whole book together much faster — but it means the notebook grows with every page.

That notebook is the model’s memory cost, and it grows with the conversation. The 25 sliding rounds only need about 1,024 tokens of notes. The 5 global rounds need the entire conversation, so they are the ones that grow without limit.

7

Reading is fast; writing is slow

There are two very different phases, and they are worth separating.

Reading the question — “prefill”

All the prompt tokens are processed at once, in parallel. A long document is absorbed in one burst.

≈ 60 tokens per second here.

Writing the answer — “decode”

One token at a time, and each single token requires reading a large part of the model again. It cannot be parallelised, because each word depends on the one before.

≈ 160 ms per token, about 6 per second.

The analogy. Reading a page is quick — your eyes take it in at once. Writing that page out by hand, one letter at a time, is slow.

This is why a long prompt comes with a noticeable pause before the answer starts, and why the answer then arrives steadily rather than instantly.

Where the experts fit in

Step 3 hides an interaction with these two phases. Every token picks 8 of the 128 experts. While reading, thousands of tokens pass the experts at once; while writing, exactly one does. That single difference changes what an expert is worth.

A prompt of 4,096 tokens makes 4,096 × 8 = 32,768 picks, which spread across the 128 experts at roughly 256 tokens each. An expert is a large block of numbers, and those numbers have to be fetched from memory before they can be used. Fetching a block once and using it 256 times is efficient. Writing is the opposite: one token makes 8 picks, so each of those experts is fetched and used exactly once — and every word repeats this in all 30 rounds, which is 240 expert fetches to produce one word.

The analogy. While reading, the hospital receives a whole clinic’s worth of patients, so each specialist stays busy. While writing, it opens for one patient, calls in eight specialists, and each of them does a single thing before going back to waiting.

Those last two numbers are the whole story of writing. Fetching the experts for a single token already fills the fast memory, so there is no room left to hold them while another token arrives. Every word is fetched nearly from scratch.

Why reading an even bigger block stops helping. It is tempting to expect that reading 4,096 tokens at a time, or 8,192, would keep improving things. It does not. The reuse only pays while the numbers being worked on fit in that same small fast memory. Measured on this model: reading in blocks of 256 tokens versus 2,048 tokens — sixteen versus 128 tokens per expert, an eight-fold increase in reuse — changed the total prompt time by about 4%. The benefit saturates long before the block sizes people reach for.

So the two phases stress the machine differently — reading gives each fetched number many uses, writing gives it one — and it is also why every expert has to be stored even though most sit idle at any moment: which eight are needed depends entirely on the words.

8

Wrapping it as a conversation

The model itself knows nothing about “messages”, “users”, or “assistants”. It sees one stream of text. Everything else is decoration added around your words before they reach it:

ideawhat actually happens
Roles (system / user / assistant)Small pieces of marker text are inserted around each message.
“Thinking”A special marker opens a private channel; the model reasons there first, then closes it and answers.
Using a toolThe model writes a structured request (“read this file”) as text. Your program runs it and pastes the result back in as text. The model never touches anything itself.
Remembering the chatIt does not. Every turn resends the entire conversation — which is why the notebook from step 6 matters so much.

One consequence: the model cannot “look something up” mid-answer unless a tool result was already pasted into its input. It only ever continues text.

✳

What it costs — and where it breaks

Once you have the eight steps, the three kinds of cost fall out naturally.

Memory

26 billion numbers must be stored. Written out in full precision that is about 104 GB; stored four bits at a time it is about 14 GB — which is how it fits on an ordinary machine at all.

The price: every number is slightly wrong.

Speed

Every single token requires reading a large part of those numbers. The machine is therefore limited by how fast it can pull data from memory — not by how fast it can calculate.

The only real lever is reading less: consult 8 experts, not 128; look back 1,024 tokens, not the whole conversation.

Quality

The approximations are tiny and usually harmless. But step 3 has an argmax in it: the router picks 8 experts by a hard “which is larger” comparison.

Nudge its input by a rounding error and it may pick a different expert. A small numeric difference then becomes a different sentence.

That last point is the deep trade-off of this whole exercise. It is why compressing the model’s memory — the step that makes it fit and run at all — is also the step that most easily damages its answers, and why the only trustworthy test of a speed-up is not “is the maths close?” but “did it still produce the same words?”

One shortcut broke that test outright: storing the conversation notebook (step 6) at low precision was fast and saved memory, but the rounding was enough to send the router down a different path, and the model fell into repeating itself. It is kept as an option and left switched off.

Words you will meet

Token
A piece of text — a whole word, part of a word, or a punctuation mark. The model’s unit of everything.
Embedding
The list of numbers that stands for a token; its coordinate in meaning-space.
Parameter
One stored number. “26B parameters” means 26 billion of them.
Attention
The matching game that lets each token gather in what earlier tokens contain.
Head
One parallel run of that matching game. This model runs 16 at once.
Expert / MoE
One of 128 specialist sub-networks per round; only 8 are consulted per token.
Router
The small calculation that picks which experts to consult. A hard choice.
Causal
Looking backwards only.
Context window
The stretch of text a token may attend to. Here, 1,024 in most rounds and unlimited in five.
Prefill / decode
Reading the prompt in parallel / writing the answer one token at a time.
Cache
A small, very fast store beside the processor. Numbers used repeatedly are kept there; everything else must be fetched from slower main memory.
KV cache
The notebook: saved attention keys and values, so past text is not recomputed.
