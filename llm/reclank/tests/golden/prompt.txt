The harbor of the town was small, but it had served the fishing boats for three hundred years. Its wall of grey stone ran out from the shore in a long curve, and at its end stood a short tower with a lamp. Each evening the keeper climbed the forty steps of the tower, cleaned the glass, trimmed the wick, and lit the lamp. Each morning he put it out again and wrote a line in the book of the tower: the time, the wind, the sea, and the boats that had come in or gone out.

The book was the oldest thing in the town. Its first pages were written in a hand that few people could read now, and the ink had turned brown. The later pages were easier. They told of storms that had broken the wall, of winters when the harbor froze, and of a summer when no fish came and the boats stayed in for weeks. They told, too, of small things: a boy who fell from the wall and was pulled out by his sister, a ship from the south that came in with a sick crew, a whale that swam into the harbor and out again before anyone could row to it.

When the town got electric light, the council voted to put an electric lamp in the tower and to end the work of the keeper. The old keeper did not argue. He said only that a lamp that needs no one will be seen by no one, and he went on writing in the book each morning, though there was no longer a lamp to light. People laughed at him for a year. Then, in the second winter, a storm cut the power lines along the coast for four days. On the second night a boat that had been out past the islands came home in the dark. The crew saw a light at the end of the wall and steered for it. It was the old lamp, lit by the keeper, who had kept oil and a wick in the tower against such a night.

After that the council put the old lamp back in its place beside the new one, and they paid a small sum each year to whoever would keep it ready. The book went on as well. Each keeper after the first wrote the time, the wind, the sea, and the boats, and many of them added a line about the weather of their own lives: a marriage, a death, a good catch, a hard year. The town now keeps the book in a glass case in the hall by the market, and a new book lies open in the tower. Visitors are allowed to read the old pages but not to touch them.

The tower itself has changed very little. The steps were repaired twice, the glass was replaced once after a gull broke it, and the door was painted blue in the year the harbor was dredged. The new lamp turns on and off by itself at dusk and dawn. The old lamp stands beside it, clean and full, and on most nights it is not lit. But every keeper climbs the forty steps at dusk all the same, and looks out at the sea, and writes a line in the book in the morning.
